WELCOME TO KINTSUGI AURELIUS

Life leaves its mark upon us all. Through loss, hardship, and the passage of time, even the strongest foundations may fracture. Yet within those fractures lies the possibility of transformation.

The Japanese art of Kintsugi teaches that what has been broken need not lose its worth. By repairing pottery with gold, each fracture becomes part of its history, a testament to all it has endured.

Marcus Aurelius understood a similar truth: while we cannot control all that life brings, we can choose how we meet it. Character is shaped through adversity, wisdom through experience, and strength through perseverance.

From these philosophies, Kintsugi Aurelius takes its meaning. Every story explores the complexities of human nature, the weight of our choices, and the enduring possibility of transformation.

For every life carries a story, every choice leaves a mark, and every fracture becomes part of what follows.

Prologue

Read prologue

Once, I was light.

Not fire, not star, but the radiance that existed before either learned to burn. My name was Uzza, and I was a Watcher. I stood on the high walls of Heaven, my wings stretched wide across eternity, and I believed myself eternal. I was fashioned to see all things and yet touch none, to carry the record of mortals to the Throne but never know the taste of their breath, the weight of their sorrow, the shape of their joy.

I thought that was glory. I thought obedience was the highest form of worship. We called it duty, but it was only sleep.

From above I watched kingdoms rise and fall, families scatter like ash on the wind, children laugh and die before the world remembered their names. I was told they were fragile dust, sparks quick to vanish. But sparks can light the dark. And in their fragility, I saw something we who were called eternal lacked.

Mortals loved fiercely because they knew their time was short.

Angels never loved at all.

I envied them, though I did not yet understand that envy.

There was a voice once, trembling in prayer, and it pierced me like an arrow. Not for herself did she plead, but for the hungry, the weary, the forgotten. Her name was not yet mine to speak, but her cry made me lean closer, my hand pressed against the veil that separated Heaven from earth.

That was the moment I ceased to be only light. That was the beginning of my shadow.

I would come to learn that Heaven does not forgive what it cannot control. That love, when turned toward flesh and soil, is the one fire angels are forbidden to kindle. And yet, once lit, it cannot be smothered.

So I confess this truth before the tale begins: I did not fall because I was weak. I fell because I was strong enough to love.

And once I loved, I was not light anymore. I was judgment.

Prologue

Read prologue

At 8:16, the kitchen clock stopped. It did not lose power. The second hand simply reached the sixteen-minute mark and stayed there while the refrigerator continued its low electrical hum and the ice maker dropped three cubes into an empty tray.

Upstairs, a faucet was running. Somebody had left it on again. Water traveled through old pipes behind the walls. The sound went from the second floor to the first, then lower, beneath the floorboards where there should not have been plumbing. A glass stood on the dining table with a thumbprint drying along its rim.

One chair had been pushed back. The others remained tucked neatly beneath the table. Nothing in the room suggested panic. The napkins were still folded. The silverware remained parallel. A half-cut lemon sat on a small plate beside a knife, its exposed flesh beginning to dull around the edges. From the hallway came the soft drag of fabric over wood. Then nothing. The clock ticked once. 8:16. The second hand moved backward. Only one mark. Then forward again. A door opened upstairs. No footsteps followed.

In the dark glass of the dining-room window, the table reflected perfectly except for the chair that had been pulled away. In the reflection, that chair was still tucked in. The faucet stopped. Somewhere deeper in the House, a child coughed. A woman answered in a whisper too low to carry through the walls. The clock began ticking normally. Outside, headlights turned between the trees. The dining-room lights brightened by a fraction. The chair beneath the window slid inward until its front legs touched the rug. By the time tires reached the gravel drive, there was nothing in the room that needed explaining.

Prologue

Read prologue

“So, how did we get here?”

I looked at the casket.

Rain had passed through earlier, leaving the cemetery grass dark and soft beneath our feet. White roses covered most of the lid. Beneath them, almost hidden, someone had tucked a single red one.

Rhonda stood closest to the grave.

Tony stayed beside her, one hand against her back.

Nobody spoke.

There are moments when silence is the only thing left that doesn’t feel like a lie.

I kept looking at the red rose.

Funny what colors become after somebody dies.

Red was lipstick.

The bottom of an expensive heel.

Something you wore when you wanted to be noticed.

Then my baby sister died wearing yellow, and suddenly colors carried memories whether I wanted them to or not.

I had been a cop long enough to think I understood death.

Crime scenes were familiar.

Reports.

Witness statements.

Warrants.

I knew how to walk into the worst night of somebody’s life and start asking questions.

That changed things.

Not all at once.

That would have been easier.

At first, I did what I had been trained to do.

I looked for evidence.

Names.

Times.

Places.

I trusted the things that could be documented.

People talked differently when they knew you were a cop.

Some didn’t talk at all.

Men became careful.

Women became suspicious.

Doors closed.

And behind those doors was an entire world I had spent years believing I understood because I had arrested people who lived inside it.

I didn’t understand shit.

She understood hotels where employees learned not to recognize faces.

Men who believed money changed the meaning of the word no.

Women who checked on one another because sometimes nobody else was coming.

She understood how to walk into a room, read it before anybody spoke, and become whatever version of herself got her safely back out.

I learned those things after she was dead.

Other things came later.

A badge could make a man nervous.

A different kind of woman could make him comfortable.

Comfortable men talked.

They bragged.

They underestimated.

They showed you exactly who they were when they believed the woman in front of them had no power.

So I learned to leave certain pieces of Detective Callaway outside the door.

The badge was easy.

Other pieces took longer.

The way I dressed changed.

The way I carried myself.

The way I looked at a man and let him decide what he thought he was seeing.

At first, it felt like pretending.

Later, I wasn’t always sure.

That is the part nobody tells you about becoming somebody else.

There usually isn’t one moment when it happens.

You make one choice.

Then another.

You walk through one door because the right door stayed locked.

Eventually, you stop looking behind you.

I went looking for whoever killed Nia.

That never changed.

But somewhere along the way, I started discovering women whose names would never have meant anything to me before.

Women who had died behind closed doors.

Women reduced to photographs, toxicology reports, accusations, rumors.

Women whose lives became less important the moment somebody discovered how they made their money.

Not because she wanted me to.

Because once I knew where to look, I couldn’t stop seeing them.

Evidence.

Courtrooms.

Convictions.

The woman who came afterward started asking a different question.

What happens when the rules know exactly what happened—

and still nobody pays for it?

The straps beneath the casket tightened.

Rhonda lowered her head.

The red rose shifted against the white.

I stared at it.

“So, how did we get here?”

The answer wasn’t simple.

Nia was where it started.

Everything after that was a choice.

Mine.

Prologue

Read prologue

The night had the feel of something about to go wrong, and Wednesday caught it sideways, half a second before it mattered.

A car sat where cars didn't sit on this block. A porch light flickered once, then gave up. The air went flat, the way a street does when it's deciding something.

She didn't trust people. She trusted the details they left behind.

Violence never knocked. It leaked.

She walked with her hands loose and her shoulders down. Trouble got skittish around people who looked bored, so she looked bored. Flat pace, eyes up, letting the block believe it was an ordinary night with nothing in it.

Behind her, a dog went off, two streets over. A door shut. Normal sounds — except they landed too far apart, with too much hush in the middle, like the night had stopped breathing to listen.

She didn't speed up. Didn't reach. Didn't turn around. That was the trick. Let it build. Let somebody think they had time to decide.

She didn't know who it was yet. Only that they were close.

The shadow came first — at the corner, thrown wrong against the streetlight. Too tall for the lamp. Too still for a man just passing through. Watching. She gave it nothing. Looking at a thing was as good as answering it.

A car door clicked shut, thirty feet on. Soft. The kind of soft that means somebody doesn't want to be heard. She kept her stride and counted — distance, angle, lag. Patience was experience. Experience was bad news.

Then the block went silent. Not quiet. Silent. No wind, no bugs, no traffic bleeding over from the avenue. That hush a street gets right before a man steps out of the dark with something in his hand and a plan he thinks will hold.

Her pulse eased down. Not nerves. Arithmetic.

Left, she had cover. Right, she had room. Behind her, they'd wish they hadn't.

Near the car, the shape shifted. Small movement. Enough.

She didn't turn her head. Didn't hand them the first look. Just walked, let them believe she hadn't clocked it, let them take one step into the mistake they'd already made.

Another footstep. Close.

Good.

She breathed out slow — the only kind of breath that cost her anything — and let the next few seconds do their work.

Violence was coming.

She got there first.

Prologue

Read prologue

Before the leak in the fellowship hall, before the bounced check in his pocket, before Kendra Blue stepped through the doors of Greater Promise Baptist Church, Reverend Taylor Smalls had already begun to come undone.

It started quietly. A tightening in his chest when he looked at the pantry shelves. A tremor in his hands when he wrote checks he knew wouldn’t clear. A hollow ache when he saw bruises on Celeste Pike’s wrist and realized he had nothing left to offer except words.

He told himself it was stress. He told himself it was temporary. He told himself God would make a way.

But the truth pressed harder each week.

He felt it in the sanctuary before dawn, standing beneath a ceiling that sagged like a warning. He felt it during prayer, when his voice faltered and he wondered if anyone — even God — was listening. He felt it when he watched families arrive hungry, ashamed, and hopeful, trusting him to be the answer he no longer knew how to give.

And somewhere in that slow unraveling, something began to watch him.

Not a figure. Not a shadow. Just a presence — quiet, patient, and impossibly aware of every crack forming inside him.

By the time the visitor arrived, dressed in gray and speaking with the calm certainty of someone who already knew his fears, Taylor was exhausted enough to listen. Exhausted enough to believe. Exhausted enough to consider the unthinkable.

She spoke of repairs. She spoke of resources. She spoke of saving his people.

And when she asked for a name, something inside him shifted — not toward evil, but toward relief. Toward silence. Toward the possibility of finally being enough.

He would tell himself he chose for the sake of the church. He would tell himself he chose for the sake of the children. He would tell himself he chose because he had no other choice.

But the truth was simpler:

He chose because he was breaking.

And broken men make dangerous promises.

Prologue

Read prologue

The first time Church called me Penny, I was seven years old and standing in his office with blood on my school uniform. I had broken another girl’s nose for telling me I did not belong to his family. He sat behind his desk, looked at my split knuckles, and asked whether I had finished the fight. When I told him I had, he brought me a wet towel and made me explain how it started.

He never told me I had been wrong to defend myself. He told me to understand what a fight would cost before I threw the first punch.

I learned that lesson well enough to become a problem he had helped create.

Twenty years later, I stood in my kitchen with my phone face down beside an untouched glass of wine, watching a local news anchor repeat the same few facts about a dead detective. The station had no name yet. Police vehicles filled the street behind the reporter, their lights washing a brick wall red and blue. By morning, the reporters would have one. Church would already know more than they did.

Mars sat across the island, still in the cream blouse she had worn to work. Her shoes lay beneath a stool, and her jacket hung over a chair. She had let herself in with the key I gave her years ago. Usually she filled a room with enough talk for both of us. Tonight she studied the television without pretending the story was unfamiliar.

She reached over and turned down the volume. “Rawlins called you yet?”

“Twice. I answered once.”

“He scared?”

“He should be more worried about what he says when he’s scared.”

Mars set her glass down. She knew what I had done with the information Rawlins passed to me.

The dead woman had followed leads I placed within her reach. Those leads had sent Church looking at people whose loyalty he had counted on for decades. A delayed meeting here, an account that did not balance there, one name arriving at exactly the wrong moment. Each irregularity had a reasonable explanation. Together, they made him question his own house.

I knew how cruel that was. I had used those memories as a map.

Church had built an empire that survived by remembering favors. That kind of loyalty could hold a family together. It could also keep an organization chained to mistakes no one was allowed to name.

I wanted what we owned to outlast his reputation. He heard every proposal as another way of saying he had grown old. Eventually, I stopped asking him to listen.

Mars watched my face while I checked the dark screen of my phone. “You can still talk to him.”

“I have talked to him.”

“You've told him what you want. That's different from telling him what you've done.”

The distinction irritated me because she was right. I could explain every decision as necessary. None of it would change the fact that I had turned people Church trusted into suspects to make him feel the weight of a system he refused to change.

A photograph stood near the window. Church sat in the center, one arm around Sera and the other resting against my shoulder. Every time I looked at the picture, I saw the same thing: the life he had given me, and the place inside it that I had mistaken for a promise.

“Sera will stay with him,” Mars said.

“I know.”

She waited, giving me room to admit what that meant. When the moment came to choose, she would remember whose blood she carried. I had never doubted that she loved me. I had begun to doubt whether love would keep us on the same side of a locked door.

The television shifted to an old photograph of the detective. I looked away before the anchor finished her sentence.

My phone lit up against the counter.

Church.

Mars saw the screen and reached for the remote. The television went silent. I answered before the second ring, suddenly aware of the cold wineglass against my palm.

He asked whether I was home, then told me to come by at nine the next morning. His voice held the same ordinary patience I remembered from childhood. I asked whether something was wrong, although we both knew he would never explain himself over the phone.

There was a pause long enough for me to hear another voice moving somewhere behind him. Then he spoke the name he had given the little girl with blood on her sleeves.

“Come see me, Penny.”

The line went dead. Mars did not ask what he said. She had seen my face, and she knew.

I set the phone beside the photograph and looked at the family Church had made for us. He had taught me to measure the cost of a fight before throwing the first punch. By nine tomorrow, I would have to tell him I had already thrown it.

Prologue

Read prologue

Before France carved his name into its soil, before the hill at Côte 188 became the place where his courage would outlive him, Freddie Stowers was simply a man who understood work. Not glory. Not destiny. Work — the kind that showed its result by sundown and left a man tired in a way that felt honest.

He didn’t know yet that the Great War would ask more of him than any field in Sandy Springs ever had.

In 1917, the world was already burning. Nations had been fighting across Europe for years, locked in trenches that swallowed men faster than commanders could replace them. Artillery thundered day and night. Machine guns carved through battalions in minutes. The war had become a test of endurance, and endurance had become a test of character.

That was where Freddie belonged.

The Army didn’t see him coming. It didn’t know that the quiet man from South Carolina would become one of its finest soldiers. It didn’t know that he would lead men through fire with a steadiness that made them follow without hesitation. It didn’t know that when the moment came — the moment that breaks or defines a man — Freddie would choose forward.

The 371st Infantry Regiment was still months away from France, still months away from the mud, the wire, the thunder of artillery that shook the ground like a living thing. But even then, something was forming among the men. A discipline. A resolve. A way of watching one another that made them stronger than the sum of their training.

Freddie felt it before he could name it.

He felt it when he corrected a strap on another man’s gear.

He felt it when someone looked toward him before looking toward the sergeant.

He felt it when responsibility began to settle on him quietly, the way leadership often does — not announced, not awarded, but recognized.

The Great War would demand heroes.

Freddie Stowers would become one.

Not because he sought it.

Not because he believed himself destined.

But because when the world finally asked him to show what kind of man he was, he answered without hesitation.

And long before he ever set foot on French soil, before he ever led a charge that would echo through history, the truth was already waiting for him:

Some men are measured by the work they do.

Others are measured by the moment they refuse to turn back.

Freddie Stowers was about to meet his moment.

Our identity

Strength is not the absence of hardship. Leadership is not simply the possession of power. Both are revealed through the challenges we face, the choices we make, and what we become afterward.

Kintsugi Aurelius is a personal literary brand built around that philosophy.

The name draws inspiration from kintsugi, the Japanese art of repairing broken pottery with gold. Rather than hiding the fractures, the art makes them part of the object's history and beauty. Aurelius evokes enduring ideals of strength, wisdom, and leadership.

Together, they represent a belief that what has been fractured can become something extraordinary.

At the center of the emblem stands the lion—a symbol of strength and leadership. Its commanding presence reflects the courage to face adversity, the responsibility that accompanies power, and the determination to move forward when the path is uncertain.

These principles shape my storytelling. My novels explore the complexities of human nature across psychological horror, suspense, historical fiction, crime thrillers, and contemporary drama. The characters confront difficult choices, imperfect circumstances, and consequences that cannot always be undone.

I believe powerful stories do more than entertain. They challenge perspectives, provoke emotion, and remain with readers long after the final page.

Behind the books

Novelist · U.S. Army Veteran · Creator of Kintsugi Aurelius

I am an American novelist, a United States Army veteran, and the creator of Kintsugi Aurelius.

My writing explores the complexities of human nature—the decisions people make, the circumstances that shape them, and the consequences they must live with.

I don't believe every character needs to be a hero, every villain needs to be entirely evil, or every story needs a comfortable ending. I am drawn to the spaces between right and wrong, where fear, ambition, love, guilt, and survival challenge what people believe about themselves.

My work crosses genres, including psychological horror, suspense, crime thrillers, historical fiction, and contemporary drama. From the supernatural world of When I Was Light to the moral reckoning of The House, the violence and ambition of Red Bottoms, and the courage found in Untitled: A Freddie Stowers Story, each novel explores a different side of the human experience.

My military background is part of my journey, but it does not define the limits of my imagination. I write about ordinary people facing extraordinary circumstances, imperfect individuals making difficult choices, and the moments that change lives forever.

I created Kintsugi Aurelius to bring these stories together under a single creative identity—stories with substance, emotional depth, and lasting impact. Stories that entertain, challenge perspectives, and give readers something to think about after the final page.

I believe some of the most powerful stories emerge from the fractures of human experience.
